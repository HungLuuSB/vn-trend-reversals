\subsection{Duration dependence of trends and market phases}

Whether the chance that a trend ends depends on its age is an old question for stock market cycles.
\citet{LundeTimmermann2004} date bull and bear phases of the US index with first-passage filters,
from 20/15\% to 15/10\%, on daily data for 1885--1997. They fit a discrete-time logit hazard with a
flexible baseline and interest-rate covariates. In their 15/10\% specification, the bull hazard drops
from five to two percent per week after six months. The bear hazard rises from five to about eight
percent per week. Rate rises raise bull hazards slightly and cut bear hazards a lot. All estimates
are in sample.

Longer samples give mixed evidence on the sign of duration dependence. \citet{Zakamulin2023} dates
63 bull and 62 bear markets in monthly US data for 1802--2019 with the Pagan--Sossounov rules. He
finds positive duration dependence in bear markets and in 93\% of bull markets, unlike the falling
bull hazard above. His dating uses a two-sided window, so turning points are known only after the
fact. Minimum phase durations also push hazards at young ages toward zero.

Both studies model the hazard as a function of phase age alone, at the index level and in sample.
Under a filter-type definition, age is tied mechanically to how far the price is from the trigger,
so an age-only hazard averages over that distance. We condition on the distance to the reversal
threshold and test, out of sample and for individual stocks, whether swing age adds information
beyond it.

\subsection{Survival analysis of price reversals and trading signals}

Survival analysis has been applied directly to price reversals, with a negative out-of-sample
result. \citet{Yao2005} treat runs of same-sign daily changes in the Australian All Ordinaries index
as spells. A single opposite day ends a run, so runs are short: up runs average 2.51 days and down
runs 2.13 days in 1980--2000. Separate Cox models use lagged price changes, measured in index points,
and the number of transitions in the previous 30 days, chosen by stepwise selection. These variables
shift the reversal rate in sample. On a two-year holdout, however, the models' Brier scores are
worse than both a random walk and the empirical survival curve at every horizon from 1 to 9 days.
The authors read this as consistent with weak-form efficiency.

Survival models also appear in stock trading, but with a different event. \citet{HuZhou2024} study
about 380 S\&P~500 stocks over 2018--2022. Their event is a return of $+5\%$ (or $-5\%$) within 22
days after a moving-average convergence divergence (MACD) or relative strength index (RSI) signal.
Deep survival models then filter the signals. The filtered strategies improve on the raw signals in
both a bear and a bull half of 2022, their only test year. The authors argue that survival data from
different stocks cannot be pooled.

Both survival studies leave open the choice of unit. Daily runs end on one opposite day, so their length is
dominated by noise, and a target return after a signal is not the end of a trend. Our swings end only
when the price moves $\theta$ against its running extreme, with $\theta$ scaled by each stock's
trailing volatility. This makes swings comparable across stocks, so they can be pooled. We
replace a single holdout with a walk-forward test over 2017--2026, and compare against a
model-free random-walk null, which echoes the random-walk benchmark of \citet{Yao2005}.

Machine-learning studies that target turning points directly report far stronger results, but
their evaluation designs leave room for look-ahead. \citet{Abolmakarem2024} predict next-day
direction, returns and turning points for four large stocks with a multilayer perceptron. They
report next-day direction accuracy of 95.8--97.0\% and a test $R^2$ of about 0.9999 for daily
returns. The data split is not stated as chronological, some targets use the full-sample minimum
and maximum, and there is no naive baseline. Accuracy of this size on daily returns is a typical
sign of look-ahead rather than of predictability. In an appendix we rebuild their targets on our data and
compare a random split with a chronological one.

\subsection{Directional-change methods}

Directional-change (DC) methods describe a price series by reversals of a set size rather than by
calendar time. A DC is confirmed when the price moves a threshold against its running extreme.
\citet{GlattfelderDupuisOlsen2011} use this event-based view to document scaling laws in
high-frequency FX data.

The closest machine-learning precedent predicts how long a DC trend lasts. \citet{AdegboyeKampouridis2021}
use 10-minute data on 20 currency pairs with fixed-percentage thresholds. They first classify whether
a DC event is followed by an overshoot, with an accuracy of 0.817. They then predict the overshoot
length from the length of the DC event alone, by genetic programming. The gain in overshoot error
over genetic programming without the classifier is not statistically significant (Hommel-adjusted
$p = 0.327$).

This work learns from completed trends only. On any prediction day the current trend is still open,
so its length is right-censored, and the prediction is not updated as the trend ages. Its only
predictor, DC length, is close to our mechanical baseline of swing age and distance. We treat open
swings as censored spells, update the hazard at each session with landmark models, scale $\theta$ by
volatility and study equities.

\subsection{Global shocks, price limits and emerging markets}

Daily price limits change how fast prices move, and so when reversals occur.
\citet{GaoJiangXiongXiong2023} find daily momentum, where the return on one day predicts the next,
in about half of emerging markets; developed markets show daily reversal. In China, removing
limit-hit days cuts daily momentum profits by about 40--60\%, so limits carry moves into the next
session. Vietnam shows daily continuation in value-weighted portfolios and daily reversal in
equal-weighted ones ($t = 4.93$ and $-5.84$).

Foreign information that arrives overnight moves the domestic opening price.
\citet{HamaoMasulisNg1990} split Tokyo, London and New York returns for 1985--1988 into
close-to-open and open-to-close parts. The foreign return moves the domestic open strongly. The
authors attribute mean spillovers into open-to-close returns to stale opening prices; volatility
spills over from New York into Tokyo's open-to-close return.

Evidence on US spillovers into Vietnam is thin and in sample. A daily study finds that the lagged
US return moves the VN-Index open in 2003--2009, but only in crisis-era sub-periods chosen after the
fact \citep{Luu2011}. A copula
study on same-date pairs finds stronger lower-tail dependence between US and Vietnamese returns after
the 2008 crisis \citep{NguyenBhattiHenry2017}.
Two annual firm panels identify a US effect from seven to nine yearly values of a variable that is
the same for every firm \citep{VoNguyen2016,NguyenHoLe2025}. None
tests out of sample.

Crisis-era comparisons of comovement are also biased. \citet{ForbesRigobon2002} show that
correlations rise mechanically with volatility while the regression slope does not. After their
adjustment, the cases of contagion in the 1997 Asian crisis fall from 15 to 1. Claims of a stronger
US--Vietnam link after 2008 are exposed to this bias. Beyond markets, the geopolitical risk (GPR)
index of \citet{CaldaraIacoviello2022} measures a further global shock; we use its daily series.

No study we know of tests, out of sample and with point-in-time timing, whether overnight global
information changes when swings in Vietnamese stocks reverse. We test this with price-limit features
in the model, and ask whether the sensitivity changed after the FTSE Russell upgrade announcement of
7 October 2025 \citep{FTSE2025}.

\subsection{Survival-model evaluation and backtest overfitting}

Our models and metrics come from survival analysis and from work on backtest overfitting, and
Section~\ref{sec:method} describes them in detail. They include the proportional hazards model
\citep{Cox1972}, dynamic prediction by landmarking \citep{vanHouwelingen2007}, random survival forests
\citep{Ishwaran2008} and discrete-time neural hazards \citep{KvammeBorgan2021}. We score predictions
with Uno's concordance index for censored data \citep{Uno2011} and the integrated Brier score with
inverse-probability-of-censoring weights \citep{Graf1999}. The deflated Sharpe ratio corrects a
trading result for selection bias, backtest overfitting and non-normality
\citep{BaileyLopezdePrado2014}. These tools say how to score a censored prediction, not how to split
serially dependent financial data. We therefore pair them with purged, embargoed walk-forward
splits \citep{LopezdePrado2018}.
