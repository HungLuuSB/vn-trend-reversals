\subsection{RQ1: Is reversal timing predictable out of sample?}\label{sec:rq1}

\paragraph{Against Kaplan--Meier: yes} The main specification \texttt{ABCDx} reaches a
year-stratified C of \CABCDx{} against \CKm{} for Kaplan--Meier, and an IBS of \IBSABCDx{} against
\IBSKm. Both intervals of H1 exclude zero ($\Delta C$ \PrimDCABCDxKm, $\Delta$IBS
\PrimDIBSABCDxKm), so H1 is supported. Every fitted model beats Kaplan--Meier on both metrics. The time-dependent AUC of \texttt{ABCDx} at five sessions is \AUCFiveABCDx, and its C
within dates, which uses only cross-sectional pairs, is \CDateABCDx, close to the year-stratified
value. The ranking skill is therefore not a by-product of differences in reversal rates between
dates.

\paragraph{Most of the skill is mechanical} The distance to the threshold alone (\texttt{DIST})
reaches C~=~\CDIST. That single variable delivers \MechShareDIST\% of the gain of \texttt{ABCDx}
over Kaplan--Meier (\GainOverKM{} in C). A swing whose price has already retraced most of the way
to its threshold needs only a small further move to be confirmed, and every model sees this. The
random-walk null, which fits nothing and only rolls each stock's own recent returns through the
labelling rule, reaches C~=~\CRW.

\paragraph{Beyond the mechanics: the pre-registered test fails} H1-mech is not supported. The
linear model on blocks A--C ranks swings better than the flexible mechanical baseline:
\texttt{ABCx} exceeds \texttt{M\_flex} by \GainOverMFlex{} in C, with interval \PrimDCABCxMFlex,
and in \YearsAboveABCxMFlex{} of the 10 test years. It does not improve the probabilities: the IBS
interval is \PrimDIBSABCxMFlex{} and contains zero. Against the random-walk null both intervals
exclude zero ($\Delta C$ \PrimDCABCxRW, $\Delta$IBS \PrimDIBSABCxRW), so the first of the two
conditions holds and the second fails. The comparison with the linear baseline \texttt{M} shows
where the calibration gap comes from: \texttt{ABCx} beats \texttt{M} on both metrics ($\Delta C$
\DCABCxM, $\Delta$IBS \DIBSABCxM), but \texttt{M\_flex} alone has a lower IBS than \texttt{M}
($\Delta$IBS of \texttt{M} over \texttt{M\_flex} \DIBSMMFlex). Letting age and distance enter
non-linearly gives the same calibration gain as the linear features. Section~\ref{sec:posthoc}
shows that non-linear models of the same features do improve on \texttt{M\_flex} in both respects.

\paragraph{Calibration} Figure~\ref{fig:calibration} plots the observed reversal rate against the
predicted probability by decile. At ten sessions the deciles lie close to the diagonal (largest
gap \CalMaxGapTen). At five sessions the predictions are too compressed: the riskiest decile
reverses at a rate of \CalTopObsFive, while its mean prediction is lower by \CalMaxGapFive. At 20
sessions the least risky decile still reverses at \CalBottomObsTwenty.

\subsection{RQ2: Does overnight global information add to local information?}\label{sec:rq2}

No. Adding block D to \texttt{ABCx} changes C by \PrimDCABCDxABCx{} and IBS by \PrimDIBSABCDxABCx,
so H2 is not supported; \texttt{ABCDx} has the higher C in only \YearsAboveABCDxABCx{} of the 10
years. The result holds in every model family (Table~\ref{tab:secondary}). Without the direction
terms the change is \DCABCDABC{} in C; in the boosted hazard and the random survival forest the
global block makes both metrics worse ($\Delta$IBS \DIBSGbmABCDGbmABC{} and \DIBSRsfABCDRsfABC).
Dropping the global features one at a time, none of the \AttribCount{} is informative after Holm
adjustment (\AttribInformative{} of \AttribCount; Appendix S1). The bank interactions with US
rates, the dollar and the dong add nothing either ($\Delta C$ \DCABCDxBankABCDx; joint Wald test
of the six terms, $p=\HTwoBankP$).

The signs of the effects are mostly as predicted, but small (Table~\ref{tab:h2signs}). The Nasdaq
return multiplied by the swing direction has the predicted negative sign in all
\HTwoYearsNasdaqRetXdir{} yearly fits: a fall in US technology stocks overnight raises the hazard
that a Vietnamese up-swing ends. The yearly fits use expanding windows that share most of their
data, so this count is not ten independent confirmations. In the full-sample fit the one-sided test
gives $p=\HTwoPNasdaqRetXdir$ (Holm $p=\HTwoHolmNasdaqRetXdir$), and neither the S\&P~500 nor the
VIX term is significant. These in-sample standard errors are, if anything, too small (see the
placebo dispersion in Section~\ref{sec:rq4}), so the signs are best read as descriptive. The Clark--West test, which allows for the
noise of estimating nested models, finds a small reduction in the five-session Brier loss from
block D ($p=\CWPABCDxABCx$, one-sided). This is a descriptive test, and the gain is too small to
show in either primary metric.

\subsection{RQ3: Do trends age?}\label{sec:rq3}

Not detectably, once the distance to the threshold is known. On its own, swing age carries
information: the
age-only hazard beats Kaplan--Meier on both metrics (C~=~\CAGE; $\Delta C$ \DCAGEKm). Given a
flexible function of the distance, adding age changes C by \PrimDCMSDISTS{} and IBS by
\PrimDIBSMSDISTS, so H3 is not supported; any gain from age is at most of the size of the upper
bound of the C interval. The linear comparison of \texttt{M} with \texttt{DIST}
gives the same answer ($\Delta C$ \DCMDIST). The share of rows that reverse within five sessions varies
much less across age buckets within a distance group than between distance groups (Appendix S1). Age-only duration dependence of the kind estimated for index bull and bear
phases is, for these swings, the distance to the threshold seen through age.

\subsection{RQ4: Has global sensitivity risen since the market-upgrade announcement?}\label{sec:rq4}

No change is detected. The coefficient on the product of the post-announcement indicator with
the global index is \HFourCoef{} ($z=\HFourZ$, one-sided $p=\HFourP$), the wrong sign for H4. The
real announcement ranks \HFourRank{} among itself and \HFourPlacebos{} placebo dates
(Table~\ref{tab:rq4}), and the joint test of ten post-announcement interactions gives
$p=\HFourWaldP$. The placebo estimates vary widely, from strongly negative to significantly
positive: five of the eight placebo dates give $|z|>2$. The date-clustered standard errors
therefore understate the uncertainty, and the placebo distribution is the more reliable reference;
the real estimate lies inside it. A single post-window of \HFourPostRows{} rows cannot separate a
change in sensitivity from ordinary variation. We report RQ4 as not detected, with low power.

\subsection{RQ5: Is the predictability worth anything after costs?}\label{sec:rq5}

No. The model exit earns a Sharpe ratio of \BtOpenSharpeModel{} at 0.40\% round-trip costs,
below the mechanical exit (\BtOpenSharpeMech), the plain directional-change exit
(\BtOpenSharpeDc) and buy-and-hold (\BtOpenSharpeBh; Table~\ref{tab:trading}). None of the
differences is significant: the interval of the model's Sharpe ratio minus that of the
directional-change exit is \BtOpenSDModelDc, and minus buy-and-hold \BtOpenSDModelBh. H5 is not
supported. The deflated Sharpe ratio of the model exit is \BtOpenDSRModel{} with the
pre-registered \BtOpenDSRTrials{} trials, and \BtOpenDSRNNineSix{} when every trading configuration
computed in the run is counted. The exit
strategies halve the maximum drawdown (\BtOpenMDDModel\% against \BtOpenMDDBh\% for
buy-and-hold), but only because they are out of the market much of the time; their returns fall by
more. The ranking is the same at the close (\BtCloseSharpeModel{} against \BtCloseSharpeBh) and at
costs of 0.20\% and 0.80\%.

\subsection{Banks: fundamentals and news attention}\label{sec:banks}

Block E brings no detectable improvement on bank rows. The bank-row model \texttt{ABCDx}, fitted on
bank rows only, reaches C~=~\BECBankABCDx; adding the
first-released non-performing-loan ratio and news attention changes C by
\PrimDCBankABCDxEBankABCDx{} and IBS by \PrimDIBSBankABCDxEBankABCDx; the IBS gets slightly worse.
The result is the same without the visually transcribed statements, with publication margins of 0
and 20 sessions, and with all news aliases (Appendix S1). Added to the
mechanical baseline instead, block E does not help either.

\subsection{Model families}\label{sec:families}

The neural hazards have the lowest IBS of all models (\IBSNnSeqABC{} for the sequence model on
blocks A--C, against \IBSABCDx{} for \texttt{ABCDx}), and the sequence model on blocks A--D beats
\texttt{M} on both metrics ($\Delta C$ \DCNnSeqABCDM, $\Delta$IBS \DIBSNnSeqABCDM). This comparison
is secondary, and its C gain over \texttt{M} is the same size as that of \texttt{ABCDx}. Reading
the last 60 sessions of prices adds nothing to the static neural model ($\Delta C$
\DCNnSeqABCDNnStaticABCD). Trees do not rank better than the logistic hazard: the boosted hazard and
the random survival forest on blocks A--C reach C of \CGbmABC{} and \CRsfABC.

\subsection{Post hoc: like-for-like comparisons and a decomposition}\label{sec:posthoc}

The pre-registered test of predictability beyond the mechanics compares a linear model with a
non-linear baseline. Its feature effects are the same at every horizon step, which may limit its
calibration rather than the features. After the final run, and labelled as post hoc, we compared
every non-linear model of the same feature blocks with \texttt{M\_flex}, re-scoring the cached
test-year predictions without refitting, with \PHResamples{} paired resamples and the same decision
rule (Table~\ref{tab:posthoc}). Of the \PHLikeCount{} non-linear models, \PHLikePass{} improve on
\texttt{M\_flex} on both metrics, among them the sequence neural model on blocks A--C ($\Delta C$
\PHDCNnSeqABCMFlex, $\Delta$IBS \PHDIBSNnSeqABCMFlex) and the boosted hazard on the same blocks
($\Delta C$ \PHDCGbmABCMFlex, $\Delta$IBS \PHDIBSGbmABCMFlex). The boosted hazard and the random
survival forest on blocks A--D do not. These comparisons are exploratory and not adjusted for
multiplicity, but they indicate that stock and local market features carry information about
reversal timing beyond the swing's age and distance, and that a flexible model is needed to turn it
into better probabilities.

The decomposition in the same table separates what is mechanical from what is fitted. The
random-walk null, which fits nothing, already improves on Kaplan--Meier on both metrics ($\Delta C$
\PHDCRWKm, $\Delta$IBS \PHDIBSRWKm). The fitted mechanical baseline adds calibration but no
detectable ranking to it ($\Delta C$ \PHDCMFlexRW, $\Delta$IBS \PHDIBSMFlexRW). The null resamples
whole days independently, so it ignores volatility clustering and price limits; part of what the
fitted baseline adds may be such regularities of the price process, rather than information in the
labelling rule alone.
